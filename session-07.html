Microservices vs Monolith

Facilitator and participant material for Day 7. Day 4 showed what happens when many machines work together: calls get slow, fail, repeat, and return old data. Today we ask the question that comes next: should the code be one program or many? We use the same order system and decide what to split, what to keep together, and how to move safely in either direction.

Opening · 0:00–0:10

A team of eight and fourteen services

A team of eight has a working monolith. It is a bit slow to release, so they split it into fourteen services. Six months later:

  • A release takes longer than before, because three services must go out together.
  • Showing one order needs nine calls, and one of them is always slow.
  • A bug crosses three repositories, and nobody can run the whole system on a laptop.

This is not a story that says "microservices are bad". They solve real problems for the right team at the right size. The question is when, and how much. Three questions for today:

  1. What exactly do we gain, and what exactly do we pay?
  2. Where do we draw the lines, so that a split helps instead of hurts?
  3. How do the pieces work together, and how do we move without breaking things?
Opening (10 min)
  1. Show of hands: who works in a monolith, who in microservices, who in something in between? Take two or three stories about the worst deploy or the worst bug, and refer back to them.
  2. Say plainly that the answer is not "microservices" or "monolith". It is a set of trade-offs, and today is about reading them.
  3. Connect to Day 4: the moment code runs on two machines, every call can be slow, fail, repeat, or return old data. Splitting a program turns many function calls into network calls.
Part 1 · 0:10–0:35

What they are, and what changes

Three words, in plain language:

WordWhat it meansData
MonolithOne program, deployed as one unit. Parts of it call each other as normal function callsUsually one database
Modular monolithStill one deployable, but inside it the code is split into modules with clear walls and public APIsOne database, but each module owns its own tables
MicroservicesMany small programs, each deployed on its own, talking to each other over the networkEach service owns its data. Nobody else reads it

What you gain and what you pay

You may gain……and you pay
Independent deploys. A team ships its service without waiting for the othersMany deploys to manage. Pipelines, versions, and the order in which things go out
Scale one part. Search gets 20 machines for the sale, the rest stays smallNetwork calls. Slower, and they fail in new ways (Day 4)
Isolated failure. A broken notification service does not have to stop checkoutNo shared transaction. Data across services is only eventually consistent
Team ownership. One team, one service, clear responsibilityHarder to see. Logs, metrics and traces across many services, or nobody knows why it is slow
Technology choice. The right database or language for each partMore to know and run. Many stacks, and a harder test setup

A function call becomes a network call

Every arrow between services is a call that used to be a line of code. Here is what changes, and each row is a problem you met on Day 4:

Function call (in a monolith)Network call (between services)
SpeedNanosecondsMilliseconds, and sometimes much slower
FailureRare. The whole program fails togetherOften, and partly: the other side may be slow, down, or unreachable
RepeatsCalled onceA timeout and retry can run it twice, so it must be safe to repeat
TransactionsOne transaction covers it allNo shared transaction. Each service commits alone
VersionsCompiled together, always matchDeployed apart, so old and new meet each other
DataA joinAn API call, or a copy of the data
DebuggingOne stack traceA trace across many services, if you built it

Three of these rows, in depth

Transactions, data and debugging are the three that surprise teams the most. Here is each one, with what breaks and what to do.

Transactions in microservices
Deep dive

In the monolithCheckout was one transaction. Either everything happened or nothing did:

checkout in the monolithone database, one commit
with db.transaction():                          # all or nothing
    stock.reserve(sku, qty)
    payments.charge(card, total)
    orders.create(cart, total)                  # if anything raises, everything is rolled back

Across servicesStock, Payments and Orders each have their own database and commit alone. There is no BEGIN and COMMIT that covers all three. If a step fails, the steps before it have already happened:

What failsWhat you are left with
The charge fails after stock was reservedStock held for nobody
The order is not created after the card was chargedA customer who paid and has no order. This is the worst one
The charge succeeds but the reply is lostA retry that charges twice, unless the charge is safe to repeat

Your options

OptionHow it worksTrade-off
Avoid the needKeep things that must change together inside one service and one databaseThe best answer when you can. It is the main reason Orders and Inventory stayed together in our worked example
Two-phase commitA coordinator asks every service "can you commit?", then tells them all to commitAll or nothing, but slow, it holds locks, and it blocks if the coordinator dies. Rarely used between services
SagaA series of local transactions, each with an undo step (Part 4)No locks held across services, but the system is only partly done for a while, and you write the undo code
Outbox + idempotencySave the event with the data, and make every step safe to repeatNothing is lost, and repeats are harmless. Works together with a saga
Rules that help
  • Put the step that is hardest to undo last. Reserve stock first, charge the card last.
  • Give every reservation a time limit (hold stock for 10 minutes, then release it automatically), so a lost message cannot hold stock forever.
  • Send an idempotency key with every charge, so a retry does not charge twice.
  • Show "pending" honestly to the user, instead of pretending the whole thing is already done.
Data: joining vs API calls
Deep dive

In the monolithShowing a customer's orders with their names is one query:

one query, one round tripthe database does the join
SELECT o.id, o.total, c.name
FROM orders o JOIN customers c ON c.id = o.customer_id
WHERE o.status = 'pending'

Across servicesOrders only has a customer_id. The names live in the Customers service, which has its own database, so there is no join. The first thing people write is a loop, and it is slow:

the N+1 trap50 orders, 50 calls
for order in orders:
    order.customer_name = customers.get(order.customer_id).name     # 50 orders x 20 ms = about 1 second, one after another
one call for all of themabout 25 ms
names = customers.get_many({o.customer_id for o in orders})     # one request, many IDs
for order in orders:
    order.customer_name = names[order.customer_id]

Your options

OptionHow it worksTrade-off
Call per rowAsk the other service for each itemSimple, but slow (N+1), and you depend on that service being up
Batch callAsk once for many IDsFast enough for most screens. Still depends on the other service at read time
Compose in a gatewayOne place (an API gateway or a "backend for frontend") calls several services and joins the results in memoryThe screen gets one call, but that layer now holds join logic and is another thing to run
Local copy from eventsListen to CustomerRenamed and keep your own copy of the namesFast and independent, but slightly old, and you must handle missed or repeated events
Snapshot at write timeStore the customer name on the order itselfBest for facts that should not change after the order. Not for live data
What you lose for good
  • Ad hoc joins across the whole company's data ("revenue by customer segment by product"). The data is now in many databases.
  • The usual answer is to send events from every service into one place built for questions like these: a data warehouse. That is the topic of the next session.
Debugging
Deep dive

In the monolithOne request is one process: one stack trace, one log file, and a debugger you can attach. Across servicesOne request visits five services on five machines. Each has its own logs, and their clocks do not agree. The question "why was this order slow?" has no single place to look, unless you built one.

ToolWhat it gives you
Correlation IDOne ID created at the edge, passed on every call, and written in every log line. Search for it and you see the whole story
Structured logsLogs as JSON with fields (request_id, order_id, service), so you can filter, not just read
Distributed tracingA timeline of one request across services, with how long each step took (OpenTelemetry is the common standard)
Metrics per serviceThe rate of requests, the share that fail, and how long they take. This shows which service to look at
pass the ID on, and log itcosts three lines per service
def handle(request):
    request_id = request.headers.get("X-Request-ID") or new_id()          # reuse the caller's ID if there is one
    log.info("placing order", request_id=request_id, order_id=order.id)   # every log line carries it
    inventory.reserve(sku, qty, headers={"X-Request-ID": request_id})     # and every outgoing call
Two more habits
  • Be able to run one service alone with fake versions of its neighbours. If you need all fourteen services to test one, you built a distributed monolith.
  • Use contract tests at each boundary, so a change that breaks the other side fails the build, not production.
Check-in 1 · 3 min

Better or worse after a split?

A shop splits its monolith into services along sensible lines. For each situation, is it better, worse, or both?

  1. Deploying a fix to the checkout without touching anything else.
  2. Showing the customer's name next to each order.
  3. Finding out why one order failed.
  4. Adding more machines for just the search part, for a sale.
  5. Renaming a field that three other teams read.
Show the answer
  1. Better. Independent deploy. This only works if the checkout service really can stand alone.
  2. Worse. The join is gone. You now make an API call, or keep a copy of the name that you have to keep fresh.
  3. Worse. The failure may be in any of five services. You need a correlation ID on every call and tracing, or you are guessing.
  4. Better. Scale only what is hot. This is one of the clearest reasons to split.
  5. Worse. It is now a contract between teams. You add the new field, keep the old one for a while, and move people over.

Notice that three of five are worse. A split trades simple code for independence, and you should only make that trade when you need the independence.

Part 2 · 0:35–1:00

Start with a modular monolith

A boundary costs very little inside one program, and you can still move it later. A boundary between services costs a lot, and a wrong one is painful to move. So draw the lines inside the monolith first, and see if they hold. Here is the shortcut that makes any later split impossible:

orders/service.py — the shortcutreaches into another module's table
def place_order(db, sku, qty):
    row = db.query_one("SELECT stock FROM inventory_items WHERE sku = %s", sku)   # Orders reads Inventory's table
    if row.stock < qty:
        raise OutOfStock(sku)
    db.execute("UPDATE inventory_items SET stock = stock - %s WHERE sku = %s", qty, sku)   # and changes it

This works today. But Inventory can never change its table, and Orders can never live in another program, because it depends on a table that is not its own. The fix is a wall and a front door:

inventory/api.py + orders/service.py — a front doorthe only thing Orders may import
# inventory/api.py  (the public front door; everything else in inventory is private)
class InventoryApi(Protocol):
    def reserve(self, sku: str, qty: int) -> Reservation: ...
    def release(self, reservation_id: str) -> None: ...

# orders/service.py
class PlaceOrder:
    def __init__(self, inventory: InventoryApi):
        self.inventory = inventory

    def __call__(self, cart):
        reservation = self.inventory.reserve(cart.sku, cart.qty)   # a plain call today
Tomorrow this can be an HTTP client with the same interface, and Orders does not change. But the failures change, because it is now a network call. Add a timeout, and an idempotency key on reserve so a retry does not reserve twice (Day 4).

Make the wall real

A wall that only exists in a document gets broken within a month. Check it automatically:

  • Public API only. Other modules may import inventory.api and nothing else from inventory.
  • Own your tables. Each module owns its tables, ideally in its own database schema, and only that module's database user can touch them.
  • One direction. If Orders uses Inventory, Inventory must not use Orders. Use an event instead (Day 3: depend on abstractions).
  • A test that fails the build. In Python, import-linter can do this. In Java, ArchUnit does the same job.
.importlinter — fails the build when the wall is brokenruns in CI
[importlinter:contract:orders-uses-inventory-api-only]
name = Orders may only use the inventory public API
type = forbidden
source_modules = orders
forbidden_modules =
    inventory.models
    inventory.service
Check-in 2 · 3 min

Spot the broken wall

These are four things that happen in a modular monolith. Which ones break a module wall?

  1. The Orders module imports inventory.api and calls reserve().
  2. The Orders module runs SELECT ... FROM inventory_items.
  3. Inventory publishes a StockReserved event and Orders listens for it.
  4. The Payments module imports orders.models.Order and reads its fields.
Show the answer
  1. Fine. That is the front door.
  2. Broken. Orders reaches into a table it does not own. This is the shortcut from above.
  3. Fine. An event is a public message, not a private detail.
  4. Broken. Payments depends on Orders' private model. If Orders changes its fields, Payments breaks. Payments should receive what it needs (an order ID and an amount) through an API or an event.

The test: could the other module be moved to another program without changing this code, apart from the transport? If not, the wall is broken.

Part 3 · 1:00–1:30

Where to draw the lines

Drawing the lines is the hardest part and the one people rush. A few rules of thumb:

RuleWhat it means
Own your dataA service is the only one that reads and writes its tables. If two services need the same table, they are probably one service
Change together, stay togetherIf a feature always needs edits in two places, they belong in one place
Split by business capabilityOrders, Payments, Search. Not "the database service" or "the UI service"
Match the teamConway's law: a system tends to copy the communication of the team that builds it. Use that on purpose, one team per service
Split for a reason you can nameA different scale, a different release speed, a different security need, or a team that is blocked

Good reasons and bad reasons

Good reasons to splitBad reasons to split
Teams keep blocking each other's releases"It is modern" or "everyone does it"
One part needs much more scale than the rest (search on sale day)"Our code is a mess." Messy code split into services is messy services over a network
One part must be isolated for security or compliance (card data)"We might need to scale someday"
Parts that release at very different speedsOne small team of five maintaining fourteen services
A clear contract that rarely changesSplitting before the boundary has been stable inside the monolith

The distributed monolith: the worst of both

If you split and keep the coupling, you get a distributed monolith: all the cost of a network and none of the independence. The signs:

  • Services must be deployed together, in a fixed order.
  • Several services share one database.
  • One change needs edits in four repositories.
  • One request makes a long chain of calls: A calls B, which calls C, which calls D.
  • You cannot run or test one service without the others.

Worked example: our shop

The shop from Day 4 has these parts. Which would you split first?

PartSplit now?Why
NotificationsYesIt is asynchronous. A failure here must never stop checkout. It reads events from a queue
PaymentsYesA third party, card-data rules, and its own scaling and audit needs
SearchYesDifferent data shape and scale. It can be rebuilt from events, so a copy of the data is acceptable
CatalogMaybeRead-heavy and cacheable. Easy to split, but ask whether you have a real problem first
Orders + InventoryNot yetThey need one transaction and change together. Splitting them forces a saga (Part 4) before you have earned it
Loyalty pointsLaterIt only needs events from Orders, so it is easy to split, but low value

Part 4 · 1:30–2:05

Working together across the network

Once there are several services, four questions come up. How do they call each other, how do they change without breaking each other, where does shared data live, and how do you keep it correct when there is no shared transaction?

1. Ask and wait, or tell and move on

Synchronous call (REST, gRPC)Event or message (Kafka, a queue)
StyleAsk and wait for the answerTell and move on
Use whenThe caller needs the answer now (is this card valid?)Others only need to know it happened (an order was placed)
FailureIf the other side is down, you are down or you waitThe message waits in the queue until the other side is back
Watch out forLong chains and slow calls (timeouts, retries, circuit breakers from Day 4)Duplicates and order (idempotent consumers from Day 4)
Chains multiply failure. Four services in a chain, each up 99.9% of the time, are together up only 99.6% of the time (0.999 × 0.999 × 0.999 × 0.999). That is about 35 hours of downtime a year instead of about 9. Every extra synchronous hop makes it worse.

2. Contracts: changing without breaking

An API between two teams is a contract. Adding a field is safe. Renaming or removing one breaks the other side. So: add new, keep the old for a while, move people over, then remove. For QEs, a consumer-driven contract test (Pact is a common tool) lets each consumer state what it needs, and the provider's build fails if a change would break it.

3. No joins across services

The Orders service wants the customer's name, but customers belong to another service. There is no join. Your options:

  • Ask by API. Simple, but now Orders depends on that service being up.
  • Keep a copy. Listen to CustomerRenamed events and keep a local copy. Fast and independent, but it can be a little old (eventual consistency).
  • Take a snapshot. Often the best answer. An order should show the name at the time of the order, so store it with the order and never look it up again.

4. A checkout across three services: the saga

In the monolith, checkout was one transaction: reserve stock, charge the card, create the order, all or nothing. With three services there is no shared transaction. A saga is a series of local steps, each with an undo step that runs if a later step fails.

Two ways to run a sagaHow it worksTrade-off
ChoreographyEach service listens for events and reacts. Orders publishes OrderPlaced, Inventory reacts, then Payments reactsNo central brain, but hard to see the whole flow
OrchestrationOne coordinator calls each step and the undo steps, and records where the saga isEasy to follow and debug, but the coordinator is one more thing to build and run

Never lose the event: the outbox

A trap: Orders saves the order, then publishes OrderPlaced. If it crashes between the two, the order exists and nobody hears about it. The fix is to write the event into the same database transaction as the order, in an outbox table. A separate process then publishes it.

orders/outbox.py — save and publish safelythe order and its event commit together
def place_order(db, cart):
    with db.transaction():                                              # one local transaction
        order_id = db.insert("orders", status="pending", total=cart.total)
        db.insert("outbox", event="OrderPlaced",
                  payload={"order_id": order_id, "items": cart.items})   # same commit as the order
    return order_id

# a separate relay process
def relay_outbox(db, broker):
    for row in db.query("SELECT * FROM outbox WHERE sent_at IS NULL ORDER BY id LIMIT 100"):
        broker.publish("order-events", key=row.payload["order_id"], value=row.payload, event_id=row.id)
        db.execute("UPDATE outbox SET sent_at = now() WHERE id = %s", row.id)   # a crash before this line means one repeat
This is Day 4 again. The relay may publish a row twice if it crashes before the update, so consumers must be idempotent: skip an event_id you have already handled. The outbox gives you "never lost". The idempotent consumer gives you "safe when repeated". You need both.
Check-in 3 · 4 min

Monolith, modular monolith, or services?

For each situation, what would you do?

  1. A team of six is building one new product and must launch in three months.
  2. Card payments must be isolated from the rest of the system for compliance.
  3. Search needs 20 times more machines on sale days, but the rest of the system does not.
  4. Forty engineers in six teams keep blocking each other's releases on one codebase.
  5. "Our code is a mess, so let's rewrite it as microservices."
Show the answer
  1. Modular monolith. Speed matters, the team is small, and you do not yet know where the boundaries are. Keep the walls clean so you can split later.
  2. Split Payments. This is a clear security and compliance reason, and a narrow contract.
  3. Split Search. A different scale is a good reason, and search can be rebuilt from events.
  4. Split along team lines. Independent deploys solve a real problem here. Start with the modules that cause the most blocking.
  5. No. Clean it up and draw the walls inside the monolith first. Messy code in services is the same mess, now slow and spread across a network.

Notice that the reasons are about teams, scale and risk, never about fashion.

Part 5 · 2:05–2:20

Moving out safely, in either direction

If you decide to split, do it slowly. The strangler fig pattern (named by Martin Fowler, after a vine that grows around a tree) replaces the old system piece by piece, with the old one still running the whole time.

  1. Put a router in front that can send each request to the old code or the new service.
  2. Pick an edge first. Notifications, search or reporting are easy. They depend on the rest, but little depends on them. Leave the core (Orders and Inventory) for last, or forever.
  3. Build the new service behind an anti-corruption layer so it does not copy the old messy model.
  4. Move the data in steps. The new service first reads through the old one's API, then keeps its own copy updated by events, then owns it. Avoid writing the same data in two places by hand (dual writes). One of the two will drift.
  5. Compare, then shift traffic slowly, with a way to go back at any time.
  6. Delete the old code. If you do not, you have two systems.
Going back is allowed. A video-quality team at Amazon Prime Video wrote in 2023 that moving one tool from many cloud pieces into a single process cut its infrastructure cost by more than 90%. The lesson is not "monoliths win". It is to measure, and to be willing to move in either direction.

Answer key — Exercise A (food delivery)

There is no single right answer. A good one looks like this:

  • Marketplace core (Restaurants, Menus, Orders, Promotions). They change together and an order needs menu prices and promotions in one transaction. It owns restaurant, menu, order and promotion data.
  • Payments. A third party, card-data and audit rules. It owns payment records.
  • Courier dispatch (Couriers and live location). Very different scale, because location updates are frequent, and a different team. It owns courier and location data.
  • Notifications. Asynchronous, and a failure must not stop an order. It reads events and owns delivery logs. Reviews can stay in the core for now.

Not split yet: Orders from Menus. They share a transaction and change together. Splitting them would force a saga before the team has earned it.

One call and one event: a synchronous call from Orders to Payments (it needs the answer to continue), and an OrderPlaced event that Courier dispatch and Notifications both read.

Talking points
  1. The difference between the designs is not the count of boxes. It is who owns the data, and whether a change in one place forces a change in another.
  2. Day 4 is the price list. Every network call can be slow, fail, repeat, or return old data. A split is only worth it when the benefit is larger than that bill.
  3. A modular monolith is not a compromise. For many teams it is the destination. For others it is the safe first step toward services.
  4. If you cannot draw clean walls inside one program, you cannot draw them between services either. The network only makes the mistake more expensive.
